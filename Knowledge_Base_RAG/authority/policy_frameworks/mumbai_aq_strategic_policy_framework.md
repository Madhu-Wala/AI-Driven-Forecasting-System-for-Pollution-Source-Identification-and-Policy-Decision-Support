## 1. Mumbai Air Quality Context

Air carries particulate matter (PM₁₀, PM₂.₅) along with gases like Ozone, Oxides of Nitrogen, and Sulfur. Particulate matter is a tiny particle (solid or liquid) suspended in the air originating from dust, smoke, and soot.

**National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for Mumbai:**
- PM₂.₅ acceptable limit: 40 µg/m³
- PM₁₀ acceptable limit: 100 µg/m³

**2024 Mumbai Air Quality Data:**
- Average annual PM₂.₅: **83 µg/m³** (approximately 2× the CPCB limit)
- PM₂.₅ levels surpassed **100 µg/m³** at various parts of Mumbai
- PM₁₀ peaked **above 700 µg/m³** around late October/early November
- Mean PM₁₀ level for Mumbai in 2024: approximately **115 µg/m³**
  - 2× the CPCB India limit (60 µg/m³)
  - 3× the EU limit (40 µg/m³)
  - 7.5× the WHO limit (15 µg/m³)
- Mean PM₂.₅: approximately **83 µg/m³**
  - 2× the CPCB India limit (40 µg/m³)
  - 3.3× the EU limit (25 µg/m³)
  - 16.6× the WHO limit (5 µg/m³)
- Average AQI has risen by **9% from 2020**, indicating a persisting problem

**Economic Context:**
- Mumbai: population over 20 million in the metropolitan region
- Contributes over 6% of India's GDP, 25% of industrial output, 70% of maritime trade
- Indian businesses lose approximately **US$95 billion annually** due to air pollution (loss of productivity, absences, premature deaths, morbidity)

**Health Context:**
- Major pollutants: PM₂.₅ and Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂)
- Health risks: dyspnoea, chronic cough, rhinitis, chronic bronchitis, high blood pressure, respiratory and ischaemic heart diseases
- The Air Quality Life Index (University of Chicago) reports average Indian life expectancy shortened by **5.3 years** compared to WHO's recommended PM₂.₅ limit of 5 µg/m³

## 2. Major Pollution Sources in Mumbai

### 2.1 Construction and Dust
- Road dust: over **30% of PM₁₀** in Mumbai's atmosphere (MPCB)
- Construction activities: contributes approximately **9% of particulate matter** (MPCB)
- Rise in high-rise construction inhibits sea breeze penetration, which normally disperses pollutants
- Last decade has seen sharpest rise in road dust due to displacement and debris during transport

### 2.2 Vehicular Pollution
- **80% of fossil fuel emissions** in Mumbai attributable to road travel (ORF)
- Vehicular pollution: **30.5% of emissions** in 2019–20 (SAFAR) — doubled from 2016–17
- Inadequate public transport has increased reliance on private vehicles
- **66% of Auto Rickshaws** in Maharashtra run without conforming to fitness and pollution norms (Hindustan Times)
- High vehicular density leads to pollutant buildup near roadways and traffic intersections

### 2.3 Waste Management and Biomass Burning
- Mumbai hosts India's largest waste dumping grounds
- Rubbish fires responsible for **11% of particulate matter** (BBC)
- **Over 27% of PM₂.₅** attributable to biofuel use (Times of India)
- Slum populations use wood, coal, and biofuels for cooking and warmth
- Decomposing waste in landfills releases noxious gases and air pollutants
- Limitations in waste treatment plant capacity perpetuate landfill reliance

### 2.4 Industrial and Commercial Activity
- Industrial sources: **18% of Mumbai's air pollution** (Times of India)
- **Over 26% of PM₂.₅** in Mumbai attributable to industrial activity (Times of India)
- Under NCAP, less than **1% of funds** dedicated to combating industrial air pollution in India (Indian Express)
- Mumbai hosts pharmaceutical factories, thermal power plants, fertilizer companies, oil refineries, engineering and electronic businesses

### 2.5 Marine Activities
- Mumbai owes **18% of its PM₁₀** and **26% of its PM₂.₅** to marine activity (MPCB)
- Busy ports with cargo ships, passenger vessels, fishing boats using fossil fuels contribute PM₂.₅, NOx, and SOx
- Old ships, lower-quality fuels, infrequent repairs worsen emissions
- National-level policies show little consideration for this emission source unique to Mumbai

## 4. Identified Policy Gaps

### Gap 1: Lack of Uniformity and Coordination
- Inconsistent benchmarks between WHO, EU, and CPCB create fragmented management policies
- No central nodal authority; agencies (BMC, MPCB, local bodies) operate in silos with lack of accountability
- India's CPCB considers AQI "Poor" at 90 µg/m³ PM₂.₅; WHO safe limit is 15 µg/m³

### Gap 2: Absence of National/Local Awareness Campaign
- No sustained national media effort educating citizens on health risks, pollution sources, or personal actions (unlike Swachh Bharat or Polio campaigns)
- Air pollution data on CPCB/SPCB portals: difficult to navigate, poorly organised, fragmented, not in formats conducive to public understanding
- Citizens lack access to user-friendly, real-time, comprehensible AQI data

### Gap 3: Inadequate Monitoring and Evaluation
- Mumbai currently has only **30 Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Stations (CAAQMS)**
- London (half Mumbai's population) uses reference-grade continuous monitors at **over 150 locations**
- Monitoring stations frequently offline for maintenance and inspections
- No comprehensive, transparent M&E framework applied to government air pollution policies
- Lack of cross-sectoral priority on air pollution limiting efficacy of mitigation efforts

### Gap 4: Poor Implementation and Enforcement
- Over **67% of India's population** lives in areas where PM₂.₅ exceeds national guidelines
- Systemic issues: weak punitive measures, limited institutional capacity, inadequate surveillance, high corruption
- Stark disparity between policy design and ground-level enforcement

### Gap 5: Outdated Regulatory Frameworks
- Regulatory frameworks rarely updated to reflect the current air quality landscape
- Broad, one-size-fits-all frameworks fail to account for Mumbai's unique profile: major ports, coastal geography, marine activities, high congestion density
- **64% of NCAP government funds** spent on dust suppression (anti-smog guns, water sprinklers) — treating symptoms rather than causes (Indian Express)

## 5. Strategic Policy Recommendations

### Recommendation 1: Shift Focus from Stopgap to Long-Term Dust Mitigation
- Divert expenditure from anti-smog guns to **stricter regulation and proactive oversight** of construction sites
- Dust mitigation measures must be planned during the scheduling stage of construction projects
- Special consideration for **pollutant-intensive activities**: excavation, demolition, and material transport
- Enforce source-control rather than post-emission suppression

### Recommendation 2: Expand Monitoring and Evaluation
- Expand CAAQMS coverage in Mumbai, particularly in high-congestion regions
- Make reliable, comprehensive, analysis-ready pollution data publicly accessible to facilitate awareness, research, and innovation
- Appoint a **licensed third-party organisation** for independent M&E assessments to ensure transparency and reduce corruption
- Use M&E results to reevaluate policies, identify deficiencies, and course-correct

### Recommendation 3: Improve Compliance and Enforcement
- Independently study, identify, and resolve problems causing low compliance rates
- Use **Regulation Impact Statements (RIS)** to evaluate policy options and their costs/benefits
- Conduct **regular and randomised unannounced audits** at shorter intervals
- Target repeat offenders with higher scrutiny and penalties
- Impose **higher fines and legal consequences** on regulatory defaulters to deter noncompliance
- Strengthen transparency mechanisms to eliminate corruption

### Recommendation 4: Adopt Stricter Benchmarks
- Current Indian NAAQS levels provide false assurance and foster complacency
- **Adopt WHO-recommended PM₂.₅ limits** (annual: 5 µg/m³) to drive more urgent and effective pollution control
- More stringent benchmarks will provide accurate health risk assessment and mandate urgent action

### Recommendation 5: Improve Transport Infrastructure
- Improve public transport connectivity to reduce reliance on private vehicles
- **Accelerate and incentivise transition to E-rickshaws** via public-private partnerships with Auto Rickshaw unions
- Shift public transport fleet to electric vehicles
- Operationalise expressways and highways to divert non-destination traffic
- Address safety concerns (especially for women) that deter public transport adoption

### Recommendation 6: Shift Policy Focus from PM₁₀ to PM₂.₅
- PM₂.₅ is a more dangerous and relevant health indicator than PM₁₀
- NCAP should prioritise measures targeting PM₂.₅ reduction at the point of origin
- Deploy **source-capture technology** at high-emission locations
- Incentivise adoption of devices that capture vehicular emissions at the source
- Establish emissions monitoring infrastructure for real-time compliance
- Target PM₂.₅ sources: industrial activities, biofuel burning, port activities, vehicles

### Recommendation 7: Adopt Eco-Friendly Construction Materials
- Mandatory environmental impact assessments of building materials in engineering/construction training curricula
- Air quality guidelines should prescribe **low-emission construction materials** starting with public sector projects
- Incentivise eco-friendly materials (stainless steel, bamboo) with policy incentives and subsidies
- Encourage solar panel installation and water harvesting systems
- Promote public-private partnerships for eco-conscious construction models

## 6. Legal Basis
In October 2024, India's Supreme Court affirmed that **breathing clean, pollution-free air is a fundamental right**. This provides constitutional backing for stronger enforcement and citizen rights regarding clean air.
